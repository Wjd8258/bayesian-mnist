# Final Report: Bayesian Neural Networks for MNIST Classification

**Author:** Jiadi Wu

**Abstract** — Standard deep neural networks are trained by point estimation, so they cannot capture uncertainty in their weights and often produce overconfident, poorly calibrated predictions when the data distribution changes. Bayesian methods place a prior distribution on the network weights, infer the posterior, and marginalize over it, offering a systematic framework for improving regularization, uncertainty quantification, and model calibration. This report first traces the development of Bayesian neural networks — from MacKay's evidence framework, through Hinton and van Camp's minimum-description-length perspective, to Neal's sampling-based fully Bayesian learning, and on to contemporary scalable methods centered on variational inference (Bayes by Backprop, variational dropout, probabilistic backpropagation) — and discusses complementary or reflective work on stochastic-gradient MCMC, deep ensembles, and cold posteriors. It then implements three models on MNIST — a standard network (MAP estimation), MC Dropout, and Bayes by Backprop — and evaluates them on accuracy, negative log-likelihood, expected calibration error, and predictive entropy across three scenarios: a clean test set, corrupted inputs and pure-noise out-of-distribution inputs, and a reduced training set. The experiments show that the measurable advantage of Bayesian methods lies in the uncertainty signal: their predictive entropy rises sharply under input corruption and out-of-distribution inputs, allowing the model to express *what it does not know*. But calibration does not improve automatically in this task — the standard network has the lowest expected calibration error, while approximately averaged probabilistic predictions tend toward underconfidence; and in the small-data scenario, Bayes by Backprop's performance is highly sensitive to prior strength. These results show that weight priors give models a route to uncertainty modeling and regularization, but their generalization benefit depends on matching the prior design to the approximation method.

**Keywords** — Bayesian neural networks; weight priors; variational inference; uncertainty quantification; model calibration; MNIST

## 1. Introduction

Deep neural networks have achieved remarkable success in image classification, speech recognition, and natural language processing, with stochastic gradient descent on point estimates of the weights at their core. But deterministic models have three intrinsic limitations. First, a point estimate gives a single weight configuration and cannot express *how certain the model is about a prediction* — in safety-critical settings such as medical diagnosis or autonomous driving, confident errors can have serious consequences. Second, the over-parameterization of neural networks makes them prone to overfitting, especially with limited training data; regularization tools (weight decay, dropout) help but usually lack a unified probabilistic interpretation. Third, modern deep networks have been shown to be systematically overconfident: their softmax probabilities often deviate substantially from their actual accuracy [1].

Bayesian theory offers a unified framework for these problems. Its basic idea is to treat the network weights **w** as random variables: specify a prior distribution p(**w**) before training to encode prior beliefs about the weights; after observing training data D, obtain the posterior p(**w**|D) via Bayes' rule; and when predicting a new sample, take the expectation over the posterior, p(y\*|x\*, D) = ∫ p(y\*|x\*, **w**) p(**w**|D) d**w** — a process called Bayesian model averaging. Compared with point estimation, the regularization effect of the weight prior suppresses overfitting; the diversity of the posterior makes predictions naturally carry uncertainty information; and model averaging avoids "betting everything on a single weight," which should improve generalization and calibration.

The theme of this review is the combination of Bayesian theory with neural networks, centered on "introducing a prior distribution on the weights so the model adapts better to different data and generalizes better." Its importance shows in three ways. First, uncertainty quantification is a foundational capability of reliable machine-learning systems: epistemic uncertainty (from parameter uncertainty) can be reduced with more data and is a key signal for active learning and out-of-distribution detection [2]. Second, the prior provides a regularization route beyond the data itself, keeping model behavior reasonable when data are scarce. Third, posterior marginalization and model averaging make predictions statistically robust to weight uncertainty, echoing the success of practical methods such as deep ensembles [3][4].

The scope of this review focuses on weight priors and approximate Bayesian inference methods themselves. The following related areas, though closely connected, are outside the scope and are not covered: Bayesian nonparametric methods (e.g., Gaussian processes, which model distributions over functions rather than weights); Bayesian optimization (which uses surrogate models to select hyperparameters, a different goal); Bayesian methods in transfer learning and reinforcement learning (extensions to specific settings); and the implementation details of probabilistic programming languages and general inference engines. These areas share the mathematical foundations with Bayesian neural networks but differ clearly in their objects of study and goals.

The rest of this report is organized as follows: Section 2 systematically reviews the history and contemporary literature of Bayesian neural networks with a cross-cutting synthesis; Section 3 describes the experimental methods and setup, including model forms, data splits, evaluation metrics, and the design motivation of the three experiments; Section 4 reports the results and interprets them against the literature; Section 5 discusses directions for the field, methods with limited prospects, and the boundaries of this work; Section 6 concludes.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Historical Background and Foundational Work

The idea of Bayesian neural networks dates to the early 1990s, motivated by reflection on two old problems in neural-network training: how to determine network complexity automatically, and how to avoid overfitting. The Bayesian view naturally embodies Occam's razor — the prior's penalty on complex models automatically trades off goodness of fit against model complexity. MacKay (1992) proposed a systematic scheme for applying the Bayesian framework to backpropagation networks [5]. Its core is the **evidence framework**: approximate the posterior as a Gaussian via the Laplace approximation, automatically determine hyperparameters such as noise variance and weight-decay coefficients by maximizing the marginal likelihood (the evidence), and compare network structures by Bayesian model comparison. This work first showed that Bayesian methods could automate the choice of regularization strength, providing an early theoretical demonstration of "how weight priors improve generalization." But its Gaussian approximation is only locally valid near a posterior mode, and it requires computing the Hessian matrix, limiting its use on large networks.

At almost the same time, Hinton and van Camp offered another line of thought from information theory [6]. Reinterpreting network training through the minimum description length (MDL) principle, they argued that a good model should encode both the weights and the data residuals in as few bits as possible — and this coding cost corresponds exactly to the KL divergence between the weight distribution and the prior. This view understands regularization as "compression" and directly yields the objective function of variational inference, making it the direct forerunner of later variational Bayesian neural network methods.

Neal's 1996 monograph founded the sampling route [7]. Using Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), particularly Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, to sample the posterior exactly, he showed in extensive experiments that even for large networks, averaging over the posterior generalizes better than point estimation on small datasets. Neal's work established in principle that "posterior averaging beats a single weight," but its sampling cost is extremely high and hard to scale to today's deep networks.

These three foundational routes — analytic approximation (MacKay), variational inference and coding (Hinton and van Camp), and sampling (Neal) — defined the three main directions of Bayesian neural network research thereafter, and they complement each other: analytic approximation is fast but crude, sampling is exact but expensive, and variational inference tries to strike a balance. Understanding this landscape is the key to the contemporary literature.

Their relative positions also frame later developments. MacKay's analytic approximation compresses Bayesian inference into a local description of a posterior mode: no sampling is needed, but the Gaussian assumption is too optimistic on the highly non-convex loss surfaces of neural networks — the true posterior is often multimodal, and a single-mode Gaussian approximation systematically underestimates uncertainty. Neal's sampling is in principle unrestricted by the approximation family and can capture multimodal posteriors, but at the cost of computation: every weight configuration needs a full gradient computation, and the Markov chain needs many iterations to mix. Hinton and van Camp's coding view redefined "posterior quality" as "description length," providing an objective function for doing inference by optimization. Most scalable Bayesian methods today can be seen as finding a compromise between MacKay's engineering feasibility and Neal's statistical completeness — and the specific form of that compromise is what Sections 2.2–2.5 unpack.

### 2.2 The Variational Inference Route

Variational inference (VI) turns Bayesian inference into an optimization problem by finding, within a restricted family of approximate distributions q(**w**|θ), the member closest to the true posterior in KL divergence. Its objective is the evidence lower bound (ELBO): maximize E_q[log p(D|**w**)] − KL(q(**w**|θ) || p(**w**)). The route revived in the 2010s as deep-learning tools matured. Graves (2011) proposed a practical stochastic variational inference method for general network architectures [8]. Using Monte Carlo estimates of the expectation in the ELBO, he unified variational inference with the minimum-description-length principle and noted that common regularization terms such as weight decay can be seen as special cases of particular variational choices. This solved the problem that variational methods had previously applied only to simple architectures, but the estimator had high variance and unstable training.

Blundell et al.'s Bayes by Backprop (BBB, 2015) is the most influential work on the variational route [9]. Its key contribution is the reparameterization trick: by moving the randomness in the Gaussian-posterior sample **w** = μ + σ∘ε onto the noise ε, the gradient of the ELBO can be computed without bias through backpropagation, seamlessly combining variational Bayesian training with the standard SGD pipeline. On MNIST classification, BBB matched dropout's performance while also obtaining a full uncertainty distribution over the weights. However, its mean-field approximation assumes weights are independent and systematically underestimates posterior uncertainty; its gradient variance is also large, and it is sensitive to the choice of prior. The balance between the KL term and the likelihood term in BBB's ELBO objective is directly set by the prior, and the choice of prior variance has a material effect on results — a sensitivity that also appears in this report's experiments and is the specific subject of Experiment 3.

To address the gradient-variance problem, Kingma et al. (2015) proposed the local reparameterization trick and variational dropout [10]. The local reparameterization trick moves sampling from weight space to hidden-activation space, where the central-limit effect sharply reduces gradient variance; variational dropout shows that the Gaussian dropout objective corresponds to variational inference under a scale-invariant prior and allows the dropout rate to be learned. This work built the theoretical bridge between dropout — the most popular regularization method — and Bayesian inference; Gal and Ghahramani then formally interpreted dropout training as a variational approximation to a deep Gaussian process and proposed MC Dropout, which estimates model uncertainty at test time through multiple stochastic forward passes [11].

Another complementary route is probabilistic backpropagation (PBP) by Hernández-Lobato and Adams [12]. Instead of reparameterization and sampling, PBP matches the first and second moments of activations layer by layer in forward and backward propagation, following the idea of expectation propagation, yielding a scalable Bayesian training procedure with very low variance. On several benchmarks, PBP's predictive accuracy and training efficiency beat the variational methods of the time, though the validity of its moment-matching assumption on deep architectures remains to be verified.

From an engineering standpoint, the variational route became mainstream in the deep-learning era because it turns Bayesian inference into an optimization problem compatible with the standard training pipeline: the reparameterization trick lets SGD act directly on variational parameters, and learning-rate schedules, batch normalization, and parallelization tools can be reused as-is. By contrast, the Laplace approximation requires computing and storing the Hessian or its approximation, and sampling methods must maintain an entire Markov chain and discard burn-in samples — both far more expensive in engineering terms than one backpropagation pass. This engineering advantage explains why most scalable Bayesian work after 2015 followed the variational direction, and why "cheap" approximations like MC Dropout — which shift the randomness of inference to the test phase so training costs exactly the same as a standard network — are more popular in practice [11].

Overall, the variational route evolved along a clear path: from Graves's generalized stochastic variational inference, to BBB's reparameterization breakthrough, to the local reparameterization trick's further control of variance, and PBP's moment-matching alternative. Their shared limitation is the expressiveness of the approximate distribution: the independent-Gaussian assumption cannot capture correlations between weights and has limited ability to approximate complex multimodal posteriors. This limitation is precisely what later work on deep ensembles and cold posteriors keeps returning to.

### 2.3 Large-Scale Sampling: Stochastic-Gradient MCMC

On the sampling route, Welling and Teh's stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD, 2011) is the key work connecting optimization and sampling [13]. SGLD adds appropriately scaled Gaussian noise to the standard SGD update; when the step size decays in a specific way, the iterate sequence converges asymptotically to the true posterior. This makes it possible to obtain posterior samples on large datasets using minibatch gradients, without storing the full data or computing the Hessian. SGLD and its successors made the MCMC route competitive again.

There is a fundamental trade-off between the variational and MCMC routes: variational methods optimize quickly and integrate easily with deep-learning frameworks, but their approximation family is restricted, and several studies have shown that mean-field variational inference underestimates posterior variance; MCMC methods are asymptotically exact but need careful tuning of step size and temperature parameters, and their mixing efficiency on deep networks is still unsatisfactory. SGLD offers a compromise: it keeps MCMC's asymptotic guarantees while exploiting SGD's engineering convenience. This report's experiments choose MC Dropout and BBB, both members of the variational-approximation family, with the MCMC route as background for identifying the sources of approximation error.

SGLD also reveals a deeper connection: the noise SGD itself injects has a specific covariance structure, and its "temperature" is jointly set by the learning rate and batch size. When the noise scale does not match the true posterior's variance, SGD's exploration does not correspond to the standard Bayesian posterior but to some "cooled" or "heated" modified posterior. This insight became an important clue in later research on cold posteriors: the implicit posterior of well-performing deep models often has a temperature other than 1, rather than being a strict Bayesian posterior [14]. In other words, the boundary between optimization, sampling, and Bayesian inference is much blurrier than it looks — the core point of the probabilistic-generalization perspective [4].

### 2.4 Uncertainty Types and Model Calibration

The applied value of Bayesian deep learning ultimately rests on the quality of its uncertainty estimates. Kendall and Gal (2017) systematically distinguished two kinds of uncertainty [2]: aleatoric uncertainty comes from noise in the data itself and cannot be removed by adding data; epistemic uncertainty comes from uncertainty about the parameters and can be reduced with more data or a stronger prior. Bayesian neural networks naturally model epistemic uncertainty, while aleatoric uncertainty can be modeled by placing a heteroscedastic distribution on the output. This framework gives clear guidance on "when Bayesian methods are needed": when the model needs to know what it does not know (as in out-of-distribution detection and active learning), epistemic uncertainty is the core signal. Calibration and uncertainty are two complementary quality dimensions: calibration answers "is the confidence trustworthy," uncertainty answers "how uncertain is the model" — together they determine whether a prediction is usable for decisions, which is why this report measures both.

Calibration is the other dimension for evaluating uncertainty quality. Guo et al. (2017) revealed the overconfidence problem of modern deep networks through large-scale experiments [1]: as networks grew deeper, wider, and used batch normalization, the gap between predictive confidence and true accuracy grew larger. They proposed expected calibration error (ECE) as the metric and showed that simple temperature scaling can only fix overall calibration, not restore the confidence ranking of individual samples. Bayesian methods, by marginalizing weight uncertainty, should in principle relieve overconfidence — which is why this report's experiments take calibration as a core metric.

The distinction between the two uncertainty types directly guides the experimental design. Out-of-distribution inputs mainly excite epistemic uncertainty — the model has never seen such data and should express uncertainty rather than force a confident prediction; input noise excites both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty. Accordingly, Experiment 2 constructs input corruption and pure-noise out-of-distribution inputs separately, to test whether the three models can convert the two kinds of uncertainty into observable changes in predictive entropy; Experiment 3 shrinks the training set from 54,000 to 5,000 to amplify epistemic uncertainty and examine the practical effect of prior constraints on model adaptability. In this way, the conceptual framework from the literature maps one-to-one onto the empirical design, so the results directly answer "in what sense do Bayesian methods improve the model."

### 2.5 Deep Ensembles, the Probabilistic-Generalization Perspective, and Cold Posteriors

Unlike methods that explicitly infer the posterior, deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017) estimate uncertainty by training several networks with different initializations and averaging their predictions [3]. This method introduces no explicit prior or posterior inference, yet it outperformed the Bayesian approximations of the time on accuracy, calibration, and out-of-distribution detection — and it is extremely simple to implement, quickly becoming the practical baseline for uncertainty estimation. Wilson and Izmailov provided a theoretical explanation for such practices [4]: many seemingly non-Bayesian successes in deep learning — SGD, early stopping, weight averaging, deep ensembles — are in fact performing implicit Bayesian marginalization or approximate posterior inference; understanding generalization requires a "probabilistic perspective," viewing training dynamics as exploration of the posterior rather than convergence to a single mode. On this view, a deep ensemble can be understood as a crude form of Bayesian model averaging, which explains why its uncertainty estimates work.

The most direct challenge to the belief that "more Bayesian is better" came from Wenzel et al. (2020) and the cold-posterior phenomenon [14]. Using HMC to obtain the "true" posterior on medium-scale networks, they found that the true posterior's predictive performance was significantly worse than point estimation and simple posterior approximations; only by "cooling" the posterior with a temperature below 1 could performance be restored. This was attributed to mismatch between the prior and the likelihood, and to approximation error. It reminds researchers that in deep networks, the absolute correctness of the posterior is not the goal — predictive performance and calibration quality are; the design of the prior (including tempering) and the choice of approximation method may matter more than "whether the Bayes formula is strictly followed."

Together, deep ensembles and cold posteriors shifted the discussion from "how to approximate the posterior precisely" to "which posterior approximation works in practice." Deep ensembles provide robust marginal predictions through diverse weight modes, and their empirical success shows that the value of model averaging does not require an explicit probabilistic framework; cold posteriors warn from the opposite side that a statistically correct posterior does not necessarily yield useful predictions — the prior and the approximation are the deciding factors. Both support Wilson and Izmailov's probabilistic-generalization perspective [4]: training dynamics, regularization, and model averaging together form an implicit Bayesian process, and understanding that process guides deep-learning practice better than mechanically applying the Bayes formula.

### 2.6 Synthesis of the Literature

Laid out on a timeline: 1992–1996, the foundations (analytic, variational, and sampling lines side by side); 2011–2015, the variational revival (Graves, BBB, variational dropout, PBP), driven by deep-learning tools making variational objectives efficiently optimizable by SGD; 2011, SGLD making the sampling route scalable; 2016–2017, the theoretical explanation of MC Dropout, the taxonomy of uncertainty types, and calibration research, turning Bayesian methods from a "theoretical ideal" into an "engineering tool"; 2017, deep ensembles offering a strong pragmatic alternative; around 2020, cold posteriors and the probabilistic-generalization perspective prompting the community to re-examine what "Bayesian" means.

Analytically, three key connections run across these directions. First, the evolution within variational methods (BBB to local reparameterization) is a progressive improvement of gradient variance — the core engineering bottleneck of variational approximation. Second, MC Dropout and variational dropout share the same mathematical foundation, but MC Dropout shifts the cost of approximate inference to the inference stage through "test-time randomness," an idea inherited by BBB's test-time sampling. Third, the connection between deep ensembles and Bayesian model averaging [3][4] turns the methodological disagreement over "whether to explicitly model the posterior" into the empirical question of "whether predictive marginalization works" — and cold posteriors [14] further show that the answer depends on the choice of prior and approximation rather than on the Bayesian label itself.

On consensus and controversy, the community broadly agrees on two points: weight priors can improve predictive robustness through regularization and model averaging; and cheap approximations such as mean-field variational inference and dropout are the workhorses of practical application. Open debates include how to choose priors (cold posteriors [14] show that a simple Gaussian prior is not optimal); how much mean-field approximation's underestimation of uncertainty harms downstream decisions; and whether deep ensembles should be recognized as a Bayesian method. These debates form the background of this report's experimental questions and explain why it reports three kinds of metrics — accuracy, calibration, and uncertainty — which correspond to the benefit dimensions emphasized by different schools.

These works also reveal gaps to be addressed: mean-field variational inference underestimates uncertainty, but more flexible posterior families are expensive; HMC-style sampling is exact but infeasible on deep networks; deep ensembles work but lack explicit probabilistic guarantees; and the cold-posterior phenomenon shows prior design is still an open problem. This report's experiments are an empirical test of the first two gaps: in the controlled MNIST setting, comparing point estimation, MC Dropout, and BBB on accuracy, calibration, and behavior under distribution shift, to examine how much weight priors actually improve generalization and uncertainty quality.

## 3. Methods

### 3.1 Bayesian Neural Network Framework

Consider a network f(x; **w**) parameterized by weights **w**, with the likelihood taken as a softmax distribution for classification. Given a training set D = {(x_i, y_i)}, the goal of Bayesian inference is the posterior p(**w**|D) ∝ p(D|**w**) p(**w**). Prediction marginalizes over the posterior: p(y\*|x\*, D) = ∫ p(y\*|x\*, **w**) p(**w**|D) d**w**. This integral is not analytically computable in practice and needs approximation. The resulting predictive distribution is the standard form of Bayesian model averaging, and its spread directly corresponds to predictive uncertainty.

This report implements and compares three representative methods. The first is the standard network, i.e., maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation: the posterior is approximated by a single weight, equivalent to minimizing cross-entropy plus an L2 regularization term (L2 regularization corresponds to a Gaussian prior) — the most common "implicitly Bayesian" baseline in practice. The second is MC Dropout: dropout is used during training (equivalent to a Bernoulli variational distribution), and at test time dropout is kept on for multiple stochastic forward passes whose softmax outputs are averaged, approximating the predictive distribution by Monte Carlo [11]. The third is Bayes by Backprop (BBB): a mean-field Gaussian variational posterior q(**w**) = N(μ, σ²) is optimized against the ELBO objective through the reparameterization **w** = μ + σ∘ε (ε ~ N(0, I)); since the prior is the standard Gaussian N(0, 1), the KL term has a closed form and can be accumulated weight by weight [9].

In implementation terms, the three methods differ in their objective functions and inference. The standard network minimizes cross-entropy plus L2 regularization — equivalent to maximizing log p(**w**|D) under a Gaussian prior — and performs a single forward pass at inference. MC Dropout's training objective is identical to the standard network's; the only difference is averaging over multiple stochastic forward passes at inference. BBB explicitly optimizes the evidence lower bound ELBO = E_q[log p(D|**w**)] − KL(q(**w**) || p(**w**)), where the expectation is estimated by reparameterized sampling and the KL term between the mean-field Gaussian posterior and the Gaussian prior has a closed form: for each weight, KL(N(μ, σ²) || N(0, 1)) = 0.5(μ² + σ² − 1 − 2 ln σ), accumulated layer by layer in one forward pass; during training the KL term is scaled by the batch-size-to-dataset ratio so the objective keeps its per-sample meaning. At test time, BBB samples multiple weight sets from the learned posterior and averages their softmax outputs. MC Dropout and BBB thus differ in training objective but share the "probabilistic averaging" mechanism at inference — the engineering expression of the Bayesian model averaging idea emphasized in the literature review [9][11].

### 3.2 Dataset and Experimental Setup

The experiments use the MNIST handwritten-digit dataset [15], with 60,000 training images and 10,000 test images, each a 28×28 grayscale image. Following machine-learning best practice emphasized in the course, the training set is further split into 54,000 training images and 6,000 validation images: the validation set is used only for hyperparameter selection (learning rate, L2 coefficient, dropout rate, prior variance), and the test set is used once for final evaluation, so that hyperparameter-tuning information does not leak into the test results. All images are standardized by the training set's mean and standard deviation; the random seed is fixed (42) for reproducibility, and model checkpoints and all numeric results are submitted with the code.

The three models share the same network skeleton: 784-dimensional input, two hidden layers of 128 neurons each, ReLU activation, 10 output classes. All use the Adam optimizer, learning rate 1e-3, batch size 256, and 12 epochs on the full data. Hyperparameters are selected on the validation set: the standard network's L2 coefficient is chosen from {1e-4, 1e-3}, finally 1e-4; MC Dropout's dropout rate is chosen from {0.3, 0.5}, finally 0.3, with 50 stochastic forward passes at test time; Bayes by Backprop's variational posterior is a per-weight independent Gaussian, with the prior chosen from {N(0, 0.1), N(0, 0.3), N(0, 1)} by validation accuracy — N(0, 1) for the full-data scenario, with 50 weight samples at test time. In the small-sample (5,000-image) experiment, BBB's prior is re-selected on the validation set, taking N(0, 0.3).

### 3.3 Evaluation Metrics

Beyond accuracy, three kinds of metrics assess prediction quality. Negative log-likelihood (NLL), −log p(y|x), penalizes both wrong predictions and uncertain correct predictions, measuring the quality of the predictive distribution's confidence. Expected calibration error (ECE) divides predictive confidence into 15 equal-width bins and computes the weighted average of the difference between mean confidence and mean accuracy within each bin: ECE = Σ_m (|B_m|/N)·|acc(B_m) − conf(B_m)|, where B_m is the set of samples in the m-th confidence bin and N is the total sample count; this measures the overall agreement between probability outputs and true accuracy. Predictive entropy H = −Σ_k p_k log p_k measures the spread of the predictive distribution, serving as a scalar proxy for uncertainty. All metrics are computed on the test set, which is used only once after all hyperparameters are fixed.

### 3.4 Experimental Design

Experiment 1 trains the three models on the full training set and reports test-set accuracy, NLL, and ECE, with training curves and reliability diagrams, to test whether Bayesian methods improve calibration while maintaining accuracy. Experiment 2 examines behavior under distribution shift: Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.3 and 0.5 is added to test images, and pure random-noise images are constructed as out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs; the change in each model's predictive entropy is measured to test whether Bayesian methods can express uncertainty when inputs depart from the training distribution. Experiment 3 reduces the training set to 5,000 images and compares the three models' accuracy and ECE, testing whether the prior's regularization effect is more pronounced when data are scarce. All three experiments share the same models and evaluation code, varying only the training-data size or the test inputs, so the results are comparable; all experiments reproduce in minutes on CPU, and the code and reproduction instructions are submitted with the report.

## 4. Results

This section reports the results of the three experiments. All numbers were obtained by the team from real runs in the local environment (PyTorch CPU, fixed random seed), and the code is submitted with the report.

### 4.1 Experiment 1: Performance and Calibration on the Full Dataset

Table 1 gives the three models' results on the 10,000 clean test images.

**Table 1 — Accuracy, NLL, and ECE of the three models on the MNIST test set (ECE over 15 equal-width confidence bins)**

| Model | Accuracy (%) | NLL | ECE (%) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Standard network (MAP) | 97.23 | 0.125 | 0.88 |
| MC Dropout | 97.43 | 0.093 | 3.30 |
| Bayes by Backprop | 94.94 | 0.184 | 6.10 |

On accuracy, MC Dropout is comparable to the standard network (97.43% vs. 97.23%), while Bayes by Backprop is about 2.3 percentage points lower. On negative log-likelihood, MC Dropout is best (0.093), MAP second (0.125), and BBB highest (0.184). NLL penalizes both wrong predictions and uncertain correct predictions: MC Dropout's lower NLL comes from its highest accuracy with a reasonable confidence distribution, while BBB's higher NLL is consistent with its more spread-out predictive distribution.

Calibration produced a finding opposite to the common expectation that "Bayesian methods automatically improve calibration": MAP has the lowest ECE (0.88%), while MC Dropout (3.30%) and BBB (6.10%) are markedly higher. Training-accuracy curves show all three models converging stably, with BBB converging slightly more slowly, consistent with the extra constraint the KL term imposes in variational training.

The reliability diagrams make this difference visible. MAP's reliability curve almost coincides with the ideal diagonal — its confidence matches its accuracy closely; the MC Dropout and BBB curves lie overall above the diagonal, i.e., mean confidence is below mean accuracy — they are underconfident. The reason is the probabilistic averaging at test time: whether averaging 50 dropout forward passes or 50 weight samples, the averaged predictive distribution is smoothed toward the uniform distribution, systematically depressing confidence. This is the opposite direction from the overconfidence Guo et al. observed in deep networks [1], showing that probabilistic averaging does not automatically improve calibration — the direction and degree of calibration depend on the method and the task.

The ordering of the three metrics in Table 1 also warns against judging Bayesian methods on a single metric. MAP has the lowest ECE but not the lowest NLL, because NLL is also affected by the spread of predictions; MC Dropout's lowest NLL comes from its highest accuracy while keeping relatively high confidence. Accuracy, log-likelihood, and calibration capture the correctness, sharpness, and reliability of predictions respectively, and they must be read together [1][11] — which is why this report presents all three.

### 4.2 Experiment 2: Uncertainty under Distribution Shift and Out-of-Distribution Inputs

As Gaussian noise on the inputs grows, mean predictive entropy rises for all three models, but by very different amounts. On clean inputs, all three models have low entropy (0.03–0.30); as noise intensity rises from 0 to 0.5, MAP's entropy rises only from 0.03 to 0.60, while MC Dropout's rises from 0.14 to 1.06 and BBB's from 0.30 to 2.06. On pure random-noise images (out-of-distribution inputs), mean predictive entropy is 1.76 for MAP, 1.94 for MC Dropout, and 2.30 for BBB — already at the theoretical upper bound for a ten-class uniform distribution (log 10 ≈ 2.30).

This shows that Bayesian methods' uncertainty signal is markedly more sensitive to how far inputs depart from the training distribution: through marginalization over the weight distribution, MC Dropout and BBB automatically raise predictive uncertainty in regions with little data support, while point estimation lacks this mechanism [2]. On accuracy, MAP degrades least under the same noise (93.5% at σ = 0.3, 90.3% at σ = 0.5), followed by MC Dropout (91.2%, 87.8%) and BBB (85.6%, 76.5%). Stronger uncertainty expression thus comes with an accuracy cost — Bayesian methods trade some accuracy robustness for sensitivity to distribution shift, and the trade-off depends on the application.

BBB's entropy is the most sensitive to noise because its posterior weights have explicit variance: weight sampling makes predictions more spread out on each corrupted image, amplifying the averaged uncertainty signal; MC Dropout's Bernoulli variational distribution only averages over a limited set of sparse sub-networks, so its uncertainty expression is more constrained. This explains the difference in how steeply the two curves rise, and it matches the direction of their entropy difference on the clean test set (0.30 vs. 0.14).

### 4.3 Experiment 3: Generalization and Calibration with Small Data

When the training set shrinks from 54,000 to 5,000, MAP's accuracy falls from 97.23% to 93.52% and MC Dropout's from 97.43% to 92.65% — both within 5 percentage points — and their ECE stays relatively low (2.61% and 5.55%). BBB, by contrast, drops sharply: accuracy falls to 87.72%, ECE rises to 20.13%, and predictive entropy rises to 0.87. For reference, at full data the three models' ECE values are 0.88%, 3.30%, and 6.10% — the speed of calibration degradation under data reduction differs just as sharply.

This reveals a matching problem between prior strength and data size. With 5,000 samples, even after narrowing BBB's prior from N(0, 1) to N(0, 0.3) on the validation set, the prior constraint is still too strong relative to the data scale: the posterior is pulled too far toward the prior, the model becomes conservative and underfits, and the predictive distribution becomes overly dispersed. This matches the cold-posterior literature's warning that "mismatch between prior and likelihood significantly affects posterior quality" [14]: the regularization benefit of a weight prior is not unconditional — prior strength must match the data scale, or it harms the model's adaptability.

The robustness of MAP and MC Dropout with small data is equally instructive. L2 regularization and dropout are, in implementation terms, lightweight implicit priors whose effective strength is set by fixed hyperparameters and does not scale with sample size. By contrast, the KL term in BBB's objective is scaled by the sample count, so when the data shrink while the prior stays fixed, the prior's relative weight automatically grows, pulling the posterior more strongly toward the prior. This suggests that the "correct scaling" of an explicit Bayesian objective can become a disadvantage with small data, while the fixed strength of implicit priors happens to avoid it — the two mechanisms suit different data-size regimes, and matching the prior to the data scale is a key design element of Bayesian modeling.

## 5. Discussion and Analysis

Overall, Bayesian neural networks are moving from "theoretical ideal" toward "engineering practice," with the focus shifting from the precision of the posterior to measurable improvements in prediction quality. This report's experiments provide direct evidence for that judgment: the value of weight priors and posterior averaging lies in the reliability of the uncertainty signal, not in free accuracy or calibration gains.

Three measurable findings deserve comparison with the literature. First, on the uncertainty-signal dimension, Bayesian methods do show a distinctive advantage: the predictive entropy of BBB and MC Dropout rises sharply under input corruption and out-of-distribution inputs, with BBB approaching a uniform distribution on pure-noise inputs — the epistemic-uncertainty mechanism at work [2]. The model can express "what it does not know," a capability point estimation lacks and a core requirement for safety-sensitive applications such as active learning and out-of-distribution detection. Second, on the calibration dimension, "Bayesian approximation automatically improves calibration" cannot stand as a general conclusion: in this experiment MAP has the lowest ECE, and probability-averaged predictions instead tend toward underconfidence. This confirms Guo et al.'s finding from the other side [1] — the calibration problem of deep networks is complex in direction, with both overconfidence and underconfidence possible, and needs explicit post-processing (such as temperature scaling) or method-level design (such as deep ensembles [3]) to correct. Third, on prior design, BBB's underfitting with small data echoes the cold-posterior literature [14]: the match between prior and data size and task difficulty is the key factor deciding Bayesian benefit, and a fixed-strength Gaussian prior lacks adaptability to data scale.

Promising research directions include prior design — how to build data-adaptive or task-adaptive priors, and the statistical interpretation of the temperature parameter, still have much room to explore. Relaxing the mean-field assumption (modeling subspaces of the weights or function outputs) could ease the problem of independent-Gaussian approximations underestimating uncertainty. The theoretical link between deep ensembles [3] and Bayesian model averaging [4] suggests combining the engineering advantages of ensembles with the interpretability of explicit probabilistic models — a pragmatic route that balances performance and principle.

For practitioners, the implication is to choose the model by the task: if the application needs out-of-distribution alarms, active sampling, or safety judgments, the uncertainty signal Bayesian methods provide is irreplaceable by point estimation; if the application only cares about average accuracy, a simply calibrated point estimate is often enough. This "choose by task" conclusion agrees with Kendall and Gal's view of uncertainty types [2] — epistemic uncertainty is decisive only in scenarios where the model needs to "know what it does not know."

Returning to this report's course theme — making models adapt better to different data and generalize better — the experiments give a layered answer. On average accuracy, the most common measure of generalization, Bayesian methods show no systematic advantage in this task: MC Dropout is comparable to MAP, BBB slightly lower. But if generalization is understood as robust adaptation to changes in data distribution and sample size, Bayesian methods provide information in the uncertainty-quality dimension that point estimation cannot: faced with unseen inputs, they actively lower predictive confidence instead of continuing to issue high-confidence predictions like point estimates. The contribution of weight priors to generalization should therefore not be reduced to improving accuracy, but understood as making the model's uncertainty behavior match its true capability — the key generalization property for reliability-oriented applications.

Conversely, some directions have relatively limited prospects. Naive full-network MCMC sampling is statistically exact but computationally too expensive at the scale of modern deep networks, and cold-posterior research further shows that the "exact posterior" itself does not necessarily yield better predictions — so it is gradually giving way to scalable approximations in large-scale applications. Naive probability averaging (uncalibrated MC sampling or weight averaging) is not a reliable calibration scheme either, as this experiment's underconfidence result demonstrates. Fixed-strength priors lack adaptability when data scale changes and need explicit prior-selection mechanisms.

This experiment's conclusions also have clear boundaries. First, it uses only an MLP architecture and the single MNIST dataset; conclusions on convolutional networks and more complex data may differ. Second, limited by compute, training epochs and network width are small, the hyperparameter grid is limited, and no control experiments such as temperature scaling or deep ensembles were run — these could change the ordering of the calibration metrics. Third, ECE measures only overall calibration and cannot reflect the confidence ranking quality of individual samples. Follow-up experiments worth doing include re-comparing calibration after temperature scaling the predictive distributions, to separate the underconfidence from probability averaging from the methods' intrinsic calibration potential; adding a deep ensemble as a fourth baseline, to test whether it is simultaneously superior on accuracy and calibration as the literature suggests; and repeating the three experiments on a convolutional network, to examine the effect of architecture choice on the conclusions. These limitations do not weaken the core observation — the value of Bayesian methods lies in the uncertainty signal rather than automatic calibration — but they counsel caution when generalizing the conclusions.

## 6. Conclusion

This report systematically reviewed the development of Bayesian neural networks from their foundations to the present, centered on the theme of "introducing a prior distribution on neural-network weights to improve generalization," and implemented a controlled comparison of the standard network, MC Dropout, and Bayes by Backprop on MNIST, with all results from real runs. The experiments show that the measurable advantage of Bayesian methods lies in the uncertainty signal: their predictive entropy rises sharply under input corruption and out-of-distribution inputs, expressing "what the model does not know." But calibration does not improve automatically — on this task the point estimate has the lowest expected calibration error, and approximately averaged probabilistic predictions tend toward underconfidence; and the match between prior strength and data size is the key deciding factor, with an overly strong prior constraint causing clear underfitting on small data. Together, these results show that weight priors provide a systematic route to uncertainty modeling and regularization for neural networks, but their contribution to "adapting better to different data and improving generalization" depends on the choice of prior design and approximation method — the central question of current Bayesian deep-learning research (cold posteriors, the probabilistic-generalization perspective). Future work can further test these findings with stronger posterior approximations, data-adaptive priors, temperature scaling and deep-ensemble controls, and larger datasets.
